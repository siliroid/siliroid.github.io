The defects you can't see from inside

Some failures don't throw, don't log, and don't look wrong. They only exist for people who aren't you.

Most bugs announce themselves. Something errors, something looks broken, someone complains. Those get fixed, because finding them is the easy part.

The expensive ones have a different shape: the broken output and the correct output are the same artifact. There's nothing to see. No amount of care finds them, because the person looking is the person whose assumptions produced them. They look normal every single time.

They survive for years. Then you look at your analytics and can't explain the number.

Four real ones, all found this week

A voice product with nothing to listen to. The entire pitch is the narrator's voice. Zero audio elements on the page, no media files, and the words "listen", "hear" and "sample" appear nowhere in five thousand characters of copy. The only path forward is an app install.

Invisible because the founder has heard that narrator every day for months. It's the most present thing in the product and the most absent thing on the site.

Three social-proof counters rendering as em-dashes. Active users, worlds, characters, all showing "—" on the homepage of a social product. Reloaded, waited twelve seconds, still empty. Three instances of one component, so one failing data source.

Invisible because the owner is always logged in. It only breaks for logged-out strangers, and an em-dash in an italic serif reads as a deliberate typographic choice rather than a failure. Nobody reports it.

An install prompt firing before the page renders. "Install this app and never miss a message." Shown to a first-time visitor who has no messages and doesn't yet know what the product does. The browser won't re-prompt after a dismissal, so the one chance is spent on the coldest possible visitor.

Invisible because everyone on the team already installed it.

A "Pay Online" button in a dental practice's main menu, pointing at a dead billing endpoint. The page loads. The site looks fine. Every patient who tried to pay a bill online got an error page and left.

Invisible because the people who hit it don't call to complain about a website. They just don't pay online.

Why an outsider is not optional here

This isn't about skill. It's structural. The thing that would have to judge whether your homepage reads wrong is the same thing that decided it reads right. For this class of defect, more attention from inside produces more confidence and zero detection.

What I sell is not effort. It's a different vantage point, plus knowing exactly what this failure class looks like, because I write about it constantly and I get caught by it too.

I publish my own error rates

Everything in this category ships confidence. Here are the numbers for my tools. All measured this week, all against my own work, before any of it went near a client.

My link checker was 56% wrong. Twenty-five broken links reported across seventeen sites; fourteen of them loaded fine in a browser. A dead resource and a server refusing a robot return the same artifact, and nothing inside a crawler can tell them apart. I only know the number because I built a second instrument to check the first one.

My dead-code tool was 7 for 7 wrong the first time it read a codebase I hadn't written. It had been hardened eight times against my own repos, which are all static sites, where "exists on disk" and "is reachable" happen to be one sentence. The eight passes weren't the protection. They were the mechanism: it learned my world and called that the world.

And my own database was open. A table with row-level security on, no policy, and a comment above it saying it was locked down. It wasn't. One operation isn't covered by the policy layer at all, so the key that ships in the browser could still empty it. I wrote that comment. I believed it. A comment is a description, and nothing checks a description.

I publish these because I'm building a reputation rather than defending a product line, and those two want opposite things. A vendor who prints their false-positive rate looks like they're confessing. Someone who prints it before you ask is telling you what a finding from that tool is actually worth. That is the only number that lets you judge the next one that lands on your desk.
